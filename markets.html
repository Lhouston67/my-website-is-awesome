Markets are influenced by supply, demand, and the number of businesses competing for customers. Explore how different market structures affect hospitals, healthcare prices, access to medical services, and the competition for skilled surgeons.

Supply and Demand

The supply of surgical care depends on several factors, including the number of surgeons, operating rooms, and staff available at a hospital. The demand for surgery comes from patients who need or choose to undergo surgical procedures. Demand can increase as the population grows or as new treatments become available through advances in medicine. These factors help explain why patients may face waiting lists, why hospitals may need more medical staff, and why the cost of surgery can change.

Market Structures

Market structures describe how businesses compete within an industry. They depend on the number of sellers, the types of services offered, barriers to entering the market, and how much control providers have over prices.

The four main market structures are perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and monopoly. Surgical care can show characteristics of different structures depending on the location and type of service.

1. Perfect Competition

Many sellers | Similar services

Perfect competition occurs when many businesses sell nearly identical products or services, with little control over market prices and few barriers to entry.

Example in Healthcare

Perfect competition is uncommon in surgery. A market with many providers offering nearly identical services could resemble this structure, but real surgical care differs in quality, specialization, location, and cost.

Fit for surgery: Very limited. Surgical services are specialized and require extensive training, facilities, and equipment.

2. Monopolistic Competition

Many sellers | Differentiated services

Monopolistic competition occurs when many businesses compete but distinguish their services through quality, reputation, location, features, or customer experience.

Example in Healthcare

Independent surgical clinics and specialty practices may compete through their areas of expertise, patient experience, reputation, and convenience.

Fit for surgery: Possible in areas with several competing practices offering different types of services.

3. Oligopoly

Few major sellers | Interdependent decisions

An oligopoly occurs when a small number of large businesses control a substantial share of a market. Each business must consider how its competitors may respond to its decisions.

Example in Healthcare

In some cities, a few large hospital systems may provide most of the hospital-based surgical care. These systems may compete over insurance contracts, facilities, specialist recruitment, and patient services.

Fit for surgery: A useful model for some local hospital markets where a few major systems dominate.

4. Monopoly

One seller | Limited alternatives

A monopoly occurs when a single provider is the only seller in a defined market and consumers have no close alternatives. High barriers to entry can help protect this position.

Example in Healthcare

A local community might have only one hospital providing a particular surgical service nearby. For that service and geographic area, patients may have very few alternatives.

Fit for surgery: Possible in specific locations or specialties, although the hospital may still face competition from providers outside the area.

Comparison of All Four Markets

Where Does Surgery Fit?

Surgery does not belong to only one market structure. The structure depends on the specific service and the local area where it is provided.

For example, an area with several independent surgical practices may show characteristics of monopolistic competition. If a few hospital systems provide most of the area's surgical services, the market may resemble an oligopoly. If only one provider offers a particular operation in an area, that service may have monopoly-like characteristics.

These structures matter because competition can affect prices, patient choices, quality, access to care, and the resources hospitals invest in surgical services.
